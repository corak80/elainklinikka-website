Video-Otoscopy – Ear Endoscopy in Dogs and Cats

Veterinarian Assaf Wydra, Eläinklinikka Saari

Ear infections are among the most common reasons dogs see a vet. A hand-held otoscope is enough for most ear examinations, but swelling and discharge can hide the eardrum. In video-otoscopy, a thin endoscope with a camera shows a magnified view of the ear canal on a monitor.

Why Video-Otoscopy?

Thanks to far better magnification and lighting than a hand-held otoscope provides, even small eardrum tears can often be seen once the canal is clean. Fine instruments pass through the scope, so examination, sampling and treatment happen under a single anesthetic.

When Is Video-Otoscopy Recommended?

Most ear infections are diagnosed at an ordinary visit. Video-otoscopy is recommended when an infection keeps returning or resists treatment; the canal is blocked with discharge or the eardrum cannot be seen; or a foreign body (such as a grass awn), a growth, a ruptured eardrum or a middle-ear infection is suspected. In cats, it is also used to remove inflammatory polyps, benign middle-ear growths that can reach the ear canal.

How Does the Procedure Work?

Badly swollen canals may first need 1–3 weeks of anti-inflammatory medicine. During general anesthesia with pain relief, a breathing tube protects your pet’s airway, because flushing fluid can drain through an open eardrum into the throat. The canal is flushed clean. Samples are examined under the microscope in our own laboratory and, when needed, sent for bacterial culture. Foreign bodies and polyps can be removed and growths biopsied. If the eardrum is intact but middle-ear disease is suspected, the vet may make a small opening in it (myringotomy) to sample and flush the middle ear. The opening usually heals within about five weeks if it is kept free of infection.

Aftercare and Possible Side Effects

We give pain relief as needed and often leave a long-acting medicated gel in the cleaned canal. The procedure, especially opening the eardrum or removing a polyp, can cause head tilt and loss of balance, a drooping eyelid and a small pupil (Horner’s syndrome, more common in cats), facial paralysis or hearing loss. These signs usually pass within a few weeks but can be permanent. If you notice them, stop any ear drops and contact us the same day. In one study, polyps removed through the scope regrew in about one in seven cats.

Chronic Ear Infections and Their Challenges

In dogs, recurring ear infections almost always have an underlying cause, most often allergy. Repeated inflammation narrows the canal, microbes shelter in a slimy biofilm, hard-to-treat bacteria such as Pseudomonas become more common, and the middle ear is often infected too. A flush clears the biofilm so medication reaches the skin of the canal, but the underlying cause must still be found and controlled.
