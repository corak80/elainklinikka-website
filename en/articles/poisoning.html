Poisoning in Dogs and Cats: Symptoms, First Aid and When to Act Straight Away

Veterinarian Assaf Wydra, Eläinklinikka Saari

Poisoning is a common reason for emergency visits to the vet. Dogs and cats are often poisoned by something harmless to us, such as chocolate, xylitol or grapes — or by human medicines. A suspected poisoning is always worth an immediate call to your vet, even if your pet still seems well: many poisons are easiest to treat before any signs appear.

Dangerous foods

Among the foods that most often poison dogs are chocolate, xylitol, and grapes and raisins; each has its own page with the dangerous amounts and signs. Onion and garlic in any form — raw, cooked or powdered — damage the red blood cells, and cats are even more sensitive to them than dogs, so never give a cat food seasoned with them. Chocolate is poisonous to cats too, though they rarely eat it, and grapes may be dangerous to cats as well. At Christmas, also keep alcohol, rising bread dough and macadamia nuts away from pets; fatty leftovers such as ham fat can cause vomiting or painful pancreatitis.

Rat poisons, medicines and other dangers

Mouse and rat poisons can cause internal bleeding, and cats that eat mice poisoned with alpha-chloralose get unsteady, twitch and become dangerously cold. Antifreeze tastes sweet, and a teaspoonful or two of it can kill a cat. Human painkillers are a common culprit: even part of a paracetamol tablet can kill a cat, and ibuprofen can cause stomach ulcers and kidney damage. Some dog flea and tick spot-ons contain permethrin, which is highly toxic to cats, even through contact with a freshly treated dog. Lilies can kill a cat: read about plants poisonous to cats. In summer, blue-green algae can poison dogs that swim in or drink the water.

Symptoms

The signs depend on the poison. The most common are vomiting, diarrhoea, drooling, loss of appetite and lethargy. Some poisons cause twitching, tremors, seizures, unsteadiness or collapse. In severe cases you may see pale, yellowish or brownish gums, rapid breathing, blood in the vomit or stools, or loss of consciousness. Xylitol can act within half an hour and chocolate usually within 6–12 hours, while bleeding from rat poison and kidney damage from lilies, grapes or antifreeze can take from half a day to a week to show. That is why you should act at once on any suspicion, rather than wait for symptoms.

First aid

Call your vet straight away and say what your pet swallowed, how much, when, and roughly what it weighs; take the packaging, or a photo of it, with you. Do not try to make your pet vomit yourself: salt and hydrogen peroxide can do serious harm. Do not give milk or oil. Give no medicine, not even activated charcoal, unless your vet tells you to. Ask your vet whether to wash poison off the coat; if so, wear gloves and use lukewarm water with a little mild washing-up liquid. On the way, keep your pet calm and wrap a cat that feels cold in a blanket. The Poison Information Centre (Myrkytystietokeskus) does not advise on animal poisonings, so always call a vet.

Veterinary treatment

Treatment depends on the poison and how soon your pet gets help. Early on, the vet can often make it vomit safely or give something that binds the poison in the gut. After that, care is about keeping your pet stable and watching it closely, sometimes in hospital. Only a few poisons have an antidote, so the sooner treatment starts, the better the outlook.

Prevention

Keep medicines, sweets, xylitol products, cleaning products and antifreeze out of your pet’s reach, never use a dog’s flea treatment on a cat, and do not keep lilies in a home or garden with a cat.
