Chronic kidney disease (kidney failure) in dogs — symptoms, stages, treatment and life expectancy

Veterinarian Assaf Wydra, Eläinklinikka Saari

Chronic kidney disease (CKD), often called kidney failure, means that the kidneys slowly and permanently lose their ability to clear waste from the blood. In dogs, it is found mainly in older animals: in a UK study of more than 100,000 dogs, about 2 in 1,000 were diagnosed with it, and nearly two thirds of them were over 12 years old. The first signs are easy to miss. But kidney failure is not an immediate death sentence — with early diagnosis and the right diet and treatment, many dogs live for many months, and some for years, with a good quality of life. Kidney disease is far more common in cats; we cover it in a separate article on kidney disease in cats. This article explains the signs, causes and IRIS stages in dogs, how we treat the disease at Saari Animal Clinic, what to feed and what to expect.

What are the symptoms of kidney failure in dogs?

The earliest signs are subtle: the dog drinks more, needs to go out more often or starts having accidents indoors, eats a little less and slowly loses weight. As the disease progresses, vomiting, diarrhoea, tiredness, muscle loss, bad breath, mouth ulcers and pale gums from anaemia become more likely. Among the 228 dogs diagnosed in the same UK study, the signs most often recorded by their vets were vomiting (half of the dogs), drinking and urinating more (44%), poor appetite (40%) and diarrhoea (37%). About one dog in five leaked urine (urinary incontinence). High blood pressure caused by kidney disease can also damage the eyes, and a dog that suddenly goes blind needs to see a vet the same day. Because the changes come on over months, they are easy to put down to old age — and creatinine, the standard kidney blood value, usually stays within normal limits until roughly three quarters of kidney function has been lost.

What causes kidney disease in dogs?

Chronic kidney disease in dogs can have many causes — inherited, present from birth or acquired later — and the original cause is not always found. In dogs, the disease can begin in the kidneys’ tiny filters (glomeruli), which then leak protein into the urine. In some breeds, such as the English Cocker Spaniel, Bull Terrier, Boxer and Chinese Shar Pei, kidney disease can run in families. This inherited form usually appears before the age of 5, and for some breeds a DNA test is available. In the same UK study, Cocker Spaniels and Cavalier King Charles Spaniels were also diagnosed with chronic kidney disease more often than crossbreeds. Gum (periodontal) disease has been linked with kidney disease too: in a large US study, the more severe a dog’s gum disease, the more often kidney disease was diagnosed later — although this does not prove that one causes the other. Kidney infection (pyelonephritis) and stones that block the flow of urine can also damage the kidneys, as can an episode of acute kidney injury, which strikes suddenly, at any age. Its causes include grapes and raisins, antifreeze (ethylene glycol), some antibiotics, a womb infection (pyometra), leptospirosis, and too high a dose of anti-inflammatory painkillers, whether human ones such as ibuprofen or the dog’s own. Leptospirosis is a bacterial infection spread in the urine of rats and other animals, for example via standing water, and it can also infect people. Acute kidney injury is an emergency: with prompt treatment the kidneys can often recover, but in studies of dogs treated in hospital for it, nearly half did not survive, and survivors can be left with lasting damage.

How is kidney disease diagnosed in dogs?

Diagnosis is based on blood and urine tests. The blood test measures creatinine, urea (BUN), phosphorus, potassium and red blood cells, and at our clinic also SDMA — a kidney marker that usually rises earlier than creatinine. In a study of 19 research dogs with kidney disease, SDMA was raised in all of them, and where stored samples allowed the timing to be checked (17 dogs), it rose on average about 10 months before creatinine. SDMA is also less affected by muscle mass, so it helps in thin, muscle-wasted dogs, whose creatinine can make the kidneys look healthier than they are. The urine test shows whether the kidneys can still concentrate urine (specific gravity), how much protein is leaking into it (urine protein:creatinine ratio, UPC) and whether there are signs of infection. We also measure blood pressure. An ultrasound or X-ray of the abdomen shows the size and structure of the kidneys and checks for stones, blockage or signs of infection. In a dog with acute kidney injury, leptospirosis should also be ruled out. A single raised value is not a diagnosis — dehydration, for example, can raise creatinine. The disease is confirmed and staged from tests taken on at least two occasions in a stable, well-hydrated dog, so we usually repeat the blood test after a few weeks.

What are the IRIS stages of kidney disease in dogs?

The International Renal Interest Society (IRIS) classifies chronic kidney disease in dogs into four stages based on blood creatinine and SDMA. Stage 1: creatinine below 125 µmol/l and SDMA below 18 µg/dl, but with other signs of kidney disease — for example, protein leaking into the urine, abnormal kidneys on ultrasound, or values rising on repeated tests. An SDMA that stays slightly raised (above 14 µg/dl) can also point to early disease. Stage 2: creatinine 125–250 µmol/l, SDMA 18–35 µg/dl; mild disease, with symptoms usually mild or absent. Stage 3: creatinine 251–440 µmol/l, SDMA 36–54 µg/dl; moderate disease, in which signs such as poor appetite, vomiting and weight loss become more common. Stage 4: creatinine above 440 µmol/l, SDMA above 54 µg/dl; severe disease with a growing risk of uraemic crises, in which waste builds up in the blood and causes nausea, mouth ulcers and weakness. If SDMA stays higher than the creatinine value would suggest, the dog is staged and treated one stage higher. Sub-stages for urine protein (UPC below 0.2 non-proteinuric, 0.2–0.5 borderline, above 0.5 proteinuric) and systolic blood pressure (below 140 mmHg normal, 140–159 prehypertensive, 160–179 hypertensive, 180 or above severely hypertensive) complete the picture. The creatinine limits apply to average-sized dogs and may differ in very small or very large ones; healthy greyhounds naturally have higher creatinine and SDMA, and sighthounds higher blood pressure, than most dogs.

How is kidney failure in dogs treated?

Kidney disease cannot be cured, but its progression can often be slowed and its symptoms eased. The cornerstones are a renal diet, phosphate binders when blood phosphorus stays high despite the diet, treatment of high blood pressure and protein loss (see below), and fresh water available at all times. When needed, we give fluids into a vein at the clinic to dogs that are dehydrated or in a uraemic crisis; some dogs with advanced disease also need fluids under the skin. Nausea is treated with anti-nausea medicine (maropitant) and a poor appetite with an appetite stimulant (mirtazapine). If the dog becomes anaemic, the cause is investigated, and anything that makes the anaemia worse, such as an infection or bleeding in the stomach, is treated. If anaemia caused by the kidney disease itself becomes marked, injections that boost red blood cell production (darbepoetin) can be used, and a dog that eats too little can be fed through a feeding tube. We never use either routinely and usually avoid them — darbepoetin, for example, can raise blood pressure — so if either is considered, we go through the benefits and risks with you. Medicines that can harm the kidneys are avoided or stopped where possible. This includes anti-inflammatory painkillers, so if your dog needs pain relief, for example for arthritis, discuss the options with your vet. Tell every vet who treats your dog about its kidney disease, as some medicine doses need adjusting. The urine is checked for infection, because a kidney infection can quickly worsen kidney function. At Saari, we build an individual plan for each kidney patient and adjust it at every check-up.

How are high blood pressure and protein in the urine treated in dogs?

High blood pressure is common in dogs with kidney disease and can damage the eyes, brain, heart and kidneys. Because excitement can push readings up, blood pressure is measured calmly and several times, preferably on more than one visit. Treatment starts when the systolic pressure stays at 160 mmHg or above, or without waiting for repeat readings if the eyes, brain or heart are already affected. Dogs usually get an ACE inhibitor such as benazepril first. If that is not enough, the dose is raised, then amlodipine is added, and telmisartan can be added as a further step. If the pressure is above 200 mmHg, amlodipine is given together with the ACE inhibitor from the start. Amlodipine should not be used on its own in dogs. If the urine protein:creatinine ratio stays above 0.5, the cause is investigated, sometimes with a kidney biopsy. The protein loss is then treated with telmisartan and a renal diet; in a randomised trial of 39 dogs, telmisartan reduced protein loss more than an ACE inhibitor (enalapril) in the first months. The aim is to lower the ratio as far as is safe — ideally below 0.5, or at least to half the starting value. Dogs whose kidneys leak protein are at risk of blood clots, and tests cannot predict which dogs will get them, so a blood-thinning medicine is usually considered too. Dehydration is corrected before blood-pressure or protein-loss medicines are started, because in a dehydrated dog they can make kidney function drop sharply. The only exception is very high blood pressure that is rapidly damaging the eyes or brain: it is then lowered while the dog is being rehydrated — gradually, under close monitoring, and not with an ACE inhibitor or telmisartan. ACE inhibitors and telmisartan can also raise blood potassium, so blood pressure, urine protein, potassium and kidney values are re-checked after each change of dose. None of these medicines is licensed for these uses in dogs (benazepril is licensed for heart failure). The vet therefore prescribes them off-label, which EU veterinary law allows, as an exception, when no medicine is licensed for that use.

What should a dog with kidney disease eat?

A therapeutic renal diet is one of the best-supported treatments. In a randomised, double-blind trial of 38 dogs with kidney failure, a renal diet reduced uraemic crises and deaths in dogs with mild to moderate disease compared with ordinary adult food, and kidney function declined more slowly. Renal diets are low in phosphorus, contain less protein than ordinary food and are enriched with omega-3 fatty acids. In dogs that leak protein into the urine, a renal diet is part of the treatment at any stage. Otherwise, start it early — ideally in stage 2, while appetite is still good — but never while the dog is ill, nauseous or in hospital. Switch gradually over several weeks, mixing the old food with the new. Give tablets in a separate treat rather than hiding them in the renal food; phosphate binders are the exception, as they are mixed into each meal as instructed. Never force a renal diet on a dog that refuses it: eating enough matters too. In a study of 116 dogs with kidney disease, dogs with a normal appetite lived much longer than dogs that had stopped eating. We sell several wet and dry renal foods at the clinic. Home-cooked renal diets must be formulated by a veterinary nutritionist: of 39 published home-made recipes for dogs with kidney disease, none met all the nutrient recommendations for adult dogs.

How long can a dog live with kidney failure?

It depends mostly on the stage at diagnosis, on protein loss in the urine, on phosphorus, and on whether the dog keeps its appetite, weight and muscle. In the UK study mentioned above, median survival after diagnosis was about 7 months, but 7 in 10 of the dogs whose stage was recorded were already in stage 3 or 4 when diagnosed, and the later the stage, the shorter the survival. In two smaller studies (27 and 116 dogs), median survival was about 15 months in stage 2, 6–11 months in stage 3, and between 2 weeks and 2 months in stage 4. These two studies and the UK one used older IRIS limits, under which stage 3 in dogs began at about 180 µmol/l instead of today’s 251. In the study of 27 dogs, those with a UPC above 0.5 lived a median of about 9 months, compared with about 19 months for the rest. Dogs with high phosphorus also did worse (about 6 versus 19 months). All these figures are medians from studies of dogs under veterinary care: half of the dogs lived longer. They are not predictions for your dog — a dog that is diagnosed early, keeps eating well and stays stable on treatment can do much better.

What does end-stage kidney failure look like in a dog?

In the final stage, the kidneys can no longer clear enough waste from the blood, and the dog becomes uraemic. It stops eating, vomits, becomes dehydrated, loses weight and muscle, is weak and sleeps most of the day. It may also have mouth ulcers and bad breath. Fluids, anti-nausea medicine and appetite stimulants can bring back good days for a while. We help you judge quality of life honestly: is your dog still eating, moving, interacting and free of pain most days? When the answer becomes no, euthanasia is the last kindness we can offer, and we talk about it with you openly and in good time.

How often should a dog with kidney disease be checked?

After diagnosis, we usually re-check within 2–4 weeks to see how the diet and treatment are working. From then on, we re-check every 3–6 months in stable stage 2 dogs, every 2–3 months in stage 3, and every 1–2 months in stage 4. A dog on blood-pressure medication should be checked at least every 3 months, even when stable. Each check includes weight, blood pressure, a blood test (creatinine, SDMA, phosphorus, potassium, red blood cells) and a urine test. Regular monitoring is what lets us adjust treatment before a small problem becomes a crisis.

Can kidney disease in dogs be prevented or caught early?

Chronic kidney disease cannot always be prevented, but it can be caught early — and treatment started early gives the best chance of slowing it down. We recommend a yearly check-up with blood and urine tests for dogs from the age of 7; ours includes the SDMA kidney test. Many causes of acute kidney injury can be avoided: keep grapes, raisins, antifreeze and human painkillers out of your dog’s reach, store your dog’s own medicines safely and give them only as prescribed. If your dog has eaten any of these, contact a vet at once rather than waiting for symptoms — the sooner treatment starts, the better. Ask us whether your dog should be vaccinated against leptospirosis. If you plan to breed from a dog whose breed is prone to inherited kidney disease, ask whether a DNA test is available.

When should I bring my dog to the vet?

If your dog drinks and urinates more than before, has accidents indoors, has lost weight or appetite, vomits repeatedly or has bad breath, book an appointment. A dog that suddenly goes blind needs to see a vet the same day, and one that has eaten grapes, raisins, antifreeze or painkillers needs help at once. If your dog already has kidney disease and stops eating, vomits repeatedly or becomes very weak, contact a vet the same day. Blood and urine tests taken at the visit show whether the kidneys are involved; to confirm chronic kidney disease and its stage, we usually repeat them once the dog is stable.
