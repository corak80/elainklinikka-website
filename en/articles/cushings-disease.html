Cushing’s Disease in Dogs — Signs, Tests and Treatment

Veterinarian Assaf Wydra, Eläinklinikka Saari

In Cushing’s syndrome, often called Cushing’s disease, the body makes too much of the stress hormone cortisol. It mostly affects middle-aged and older dogs and comes on slowly, so it is easily mistaken for ageing. Most cases are caused by a small, usually benign growth in the brain’s pituitary gland; fewer by a growth in an adrenal gland. Long-term steroid medicine can cause the same signs; never stop it suddenly without asking us.

Signs

Drinking and peeing a lot (sometimes with accidents indoors), a big appetite, a pot belly, panting, weak muscles, thinning hair on both sides of the body, thin skin, and repeated skin or urinary infections.

Call at Once

Call us on (06) 321 7300 at once if your dog suddenly struggles to breathe, collapses or has a seizure. If a dog on medicine for Cushing’s becomes dull or weak, stops eating, vomits or has diarrhoea, stop that medicine and call us at once. Outside opening hours, call the regional emergency vet on 0600 399 299.

Tests

The first test is simple and inexpensive: a morning urine sample that you collect at home and bring to us. For at least 24 hours before, preferably 48, keep your dog’s routine calm and normal, with nothing special: stress can raise the result and give a false alarm. A normal result makes Cushing’s unlikely. A raised result does not prove it, so blood tests follow: either two samples about an hour apart, or several over about eight hours while your dog stays in day care. The samples go to an outside laboratory, so results take a few days. Tell us about any steroid medicine, even drops or creams, as it can affect the results.

Treatment

Treatment is usually a daily medicine for life, with regular check-ups and blood tests to adjust the dose. Drinking and appetite often improve within weeks; the coat can take months. An adrenal growth can sometimes be removed surgically at a referral hospital.

Without Treatment

Untreated, the signs usually continue and can slowly get worse, and complications such as infections, diabetes, high blood pressure and blood clots can develop. Treatment usually starts when the signs affect your dog’s quality of life or complications appear; mild cases can sometimes just be monitored. We decide together with you.
