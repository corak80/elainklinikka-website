Grapes and Raisins – Why They Are Dangerous for Dogs

Veterinarian Assaf Wydra, Eläinklinikka Saari

Grapes and raisins can cause sudden kidney failure in dogs. No amount is known to be safe: some dogs fall ill after only a few, while others eat many without harm, and there is no way to tell in advance. If your dog has eaten any, call your vet straight away, even if it seems fine.

Where grapes and raisins hide

Fresh grapes of any colour, raisins, sultanas and currants (dried grapes) are all dangerous. Raisins are easy to miss: they hide in Christmas cake, raisin buns, muesli and nut-and-raisin mixes, and go into glögi along with almonds.

Why are they dangerous?

Researchers suspect that the harmful substance is tartaric acid, to which dogs are especially sensitive. The amount varies from grape to grape and from batch to batch, which may explain why some dogs are fine and others are not. The poison damages the kidneys, and the damage can be permanent or fatal. Cats may be at risk too, so call your vet if your cat has eaten grapes or raisins.

Signs

Vomiting is often the first sign, usually within 6–12 hours, followed by diarrhoea, tiredness and loss of appetite. Kidney failure develops over one to three days: the dog may pass much less urine, or none at all. A dog that seems fine the next day may still be in danger, which is why treatment should start before any signs appear.

What to do and how it is treated

Call your vet straight away and say how many grapes or raisins your dog ate, when, and roughly what it weighs. Do not try to make your dog vomit yourself. If your dog comes in early, the vet makes it vomit safely and may give something that binds the poison in the gut. Some dogs then need a drip for two days or more, with blood tests to check the kidneys. With early treatment the outlook is good, but once the kidneys have stopped working it is poor. Outside opening hours, call the regional out-of-hours emergency number, 0600 399 299. Read more about other poisons in our poisoning article.
