## intro

**C1. "one of the most common emergencies in intact bitches"** → **VERIFIED**. Jitpean 2014: "One of the most common diseases in intact bitches is pyometra — a potentially life-threatening disease". Hagman 2022: "considered a medical emergency".

**C2. "about one in four unspayed female dogs develops pyometra by the age of 10"** → **IMPRECISE**. The 1-in-4 figure is Egenvall 2001 ("on average 23–24% of the bitches in the databases will have experienced pyometra by 10 years of age"), from 1995–96 Swedish insurance claims. The larger, later dataset (Jitpean 2012, 1995–2006, >260,000 bitches) gives **19%** by age 10, and Hagman's own review states "In Sweden, an average **20%** of all bitches are diagnosed before 10 years of age and more than 50% in certain high-risk breeds." The population is *Swedish insured bitches* (mostly intact, since elective spaying is uncommon in Sweden), not "unspayed dogs" in general, and the breed range is 3–66%. "One in four" is the upper bound of the older study.
Suggested: *"roughly one in five unspayed bitches (19–24 % in large Swedish insurance studies) develops pyometra by the age of 10 — and more than half in some high-risk breeds"*.

**C3. "it also occurs in cats"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2014 cats; Hagman 2022.

**C4. "usually needs emergency surgery"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2022: "Surgical ovariohysterectomy is the safest and most effective treatment."

## symptoms.text

**C5. Open pyometra = discharge, licking; closed = sicker, vaguer signs; PU/PD, anorexia, lethargy, vomiting, swollen/painful belly, fever, hypothermia in severe cases** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2022 Table 4: fever 32–50%, hypothermia 3–10%, distended abdomen 5%, pain on palpation 23–80%; classic signs anorexia, depression, PD/PU, vomiting. Jitpean 2017: closed-cervix dogs more often moderately/severely depressed (56% vs 30%), more sepsis (77% vs 51%), more leukocytosis (89% vs 62%).
Minor: "swollen belly" is uncommon (distended abdomen only 5% in Hagman's table) — consider "sometimes a swollen or painful belly".

**C6. "see a vet the same day"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2022: "seek immediate veterinary care… a patient's status may deteriorate rapidly".

## onset.text

**C7. "Pyometra typically develops 2–8 weeks after a heat"** → **IMPRECISE**. Hagman 2022: "usually diagnosed within 4 months of estrus"; "presented up to 2 months to 4 months after estrus"; also "the disease can occur at any time during the estrus cycle." Cornell: "one to two months after their previous heat cycle." Jitpean 2014 cohort: "All bitches were diagnosed within 2 months of the previous oestrus." So 2–8 weeks is the typical peak but the window is wider.
Suggested: *"Pyometra typically shows itself 1–2 months after a heat, and nearly always within 4 months of it"* — and change "in the weeks after her heat" (symptoms, diagnosis, contact sections) to "in the weeks and months after her heat".

**C8. "bacteria — usually E. coli from the dog's own gut — climb up through the cervix"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2022: "The pathogen most often isolated from pyometra uteri is Escherichia coli"; ascending infection from the vagina; strains match faecal/UTI isolates (Hagman 2022 refs on faecal-origin E. coli). Merck: E. coli most common, opportunistic organisms from the vagina. Cornell: same.

**C9. "a closed pyometra can take a dog from 'a bit off' to collapse within a day or two"** → **UNVERIFIABLE / IMPRECISE**. No source gives a time-to-collapse. Hagman 2022 says only "may deteriorate rapidly". Jitpean 2017 found closed cervix = more severe illness *at admission* but **not** poorer outcome; all 5 deaths in that cohort were open-cervix dogs. "Within a day or two" is an invented figure.
Suggested: *"a closed pyometra can make a dog seriously ill within days: toxins and bacteria pass from the uterus into the bloodstream and about six in ten bitches with pyometra already have sepsis when they arrive"* (Hagman 2022: "Approximately 60% of bitches and 86% of queens with pyometra suffer from sepsis").

**C10. "toxins and bacteria pass from the uterus into the bloodstream"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2018 abstract: "endotoxemia and sepsis often are induced".

**C11. "the sooner surgery is done, the better the outcome"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2018 abstract: "Early diagnosis and treatment are vital to increase chances of survival."

## causes.text

**C12. Hormonal cause; repeated progesterone cycles → cystic endometrial hyperplasia → bacterial breeding ground** → **VERIFIED** (classical model). Merck: CEH progesterone-mediated, "usually precedes the development of pyometra, although it does not always progress to pyometra"; Hagman 2022: progesterone plays the key role. (Hagman notes CEH is not obligatory — the text's "over the years these repeated cycles cause…" is acceptable simplification.)

**C13. "median age is around 7 years"** → **IMPRECISE**. Jitpean 2012: *mean* age at diagnosis 7.0 years (SD 2.2) in insurance data. Hospital cohorts are older: Jitpean 2014 mean 9 ± 1.4 y; Jitpean 2017 median 9.0 (open) / 9.6 (closed); **Finnish** case-control (Niskanen 1998) median 9 years. No source gives a median of 7.
Suggested: *"the average age at diagnosis is about 7 years, and most patients are 6–10 years old"* or, using the Finnish data, *"in Finnish dogs the median age at diagnosis is 9 years"*.

**C14. "but it can occur after the first heat"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2022: reported from 4 months to 18 years; Niskanen 1998: cases from 9 months.

**C15. "Hormone treatments given to suppress heat or terminate pregnancy increase the risk"** → **IMPRECISE (evidence is asymmetric)**. Hagman 2022: "Exogenous treatments with steroid hormones, such as progestogens, or estrogen compounds that increase the response to progesterone are associated with increased risk." Merck: estrogen increases progesterone receptors. BUT the Finnish case-control (Niskanen 1998, 953 cases) found: oestrogen (mismating injection) raised risk in bitches ≤4 y (OR > 6) while "No significant risk-enhancing effect of progestin treatment was detected and it is unlikely that such a risk, if present, is high." Also Niskanen: nulliparous bitches had higher risk (adjusted OR 6.63) — pregnancy is mildly protective (Hagman 2022 agrees).
Suggested: *"Hormone injections to terminate an unwanted pregnancy (oestrogens) clearly increase the risk, especially in young bitches; heat-suppression hormones (progestogens) are also considered a risk factor, although a large Finnish study did not confirm a strong effect."* Same nuance needed in prevention.text.

**C16. "Some breeds, such as the Golden Retriever, Rottweiler, Bernese Mountain Dog and Cavalier King Charles Spaniel, are affected more often"** → **VERIFIED but INCOMPLETE**. All four are in Egenvall 2001's multivariable high-risk list (rough Collie, Rottweiler, CKCS, Golden Retriever, Bernese Mountain Dog, English Cocker Spaniel), and Hagman 2022 calls the Golden Retriever "a breed that has increased risk" (ABCC4 locus). However the larger Jitpean 2012 dataset (Hagman 2022 Table 1) ranks the top-10 by proportion affected before 10 y as: **Bernese Mountain Dog 66%, Great Dane 62%, Leonberger 61%, Rottweiler 58%, Irish Wolfhound 58%, Staffordshire Bull Terrier 54%, Keeshond 52%, Bull Terrier 52%, Bouvier des Flandres 50%, Newfoundland 50%** — Golden Retriever and CKCS are not in that top 10. Documented **low-risk** breeds: Finnish Spitz 3%, Norrbotten Spitz 4%, Coton de Tulear 5%, Maltese 8%, Gordon Setter 8%, Laika 8%, Saluki 10%, Tibetan Terrier 10% (Jitpean 2012/Hagman Table 1); Drever, German Shepherd, Dachshunds, Swedish Hound (Egenvall 2001); wire-haired Dachshund and mongrels (Niskanen 1998, Finnish data).
Suggested: *"Some breeds are affected far more often — in Swedish insurance data more than half of Bernese Mountain Dogs, Great Danes, Leonbergers, Rottweilers and Irish Wolfhounds develop pyometra by age 10, and Golden Retrievers and Cavalier King Charles Spaniels are also above average — while the Finnish Spitz, Dachshunds, German Shepherds and mixed-breed dogs are at lower risk. No breed is safe."*

## selfheal.text

**C17. "Untreated pyometra is almost always fatal"** → **IMPRECISE**. Sources say "potentially life-threatening", "deadly consequences if left untreated" (Jitpean 2014 intro), but no study quantifies the untreated course, and open-cervix cases can smoulder. Hagman 2022 even notes "Antimicrobials alone … may reduce the disease and prevent its progression but does not result in uterine healing."
Suggested: *"Untreated pyometra is life-threatening: the uterus can rupture, and the infection leads to sepsis, kidney damage and peritonitis."*

**C18. "the uterus can rupture, and the infection causes sepsis, kidney failure and peritonitis"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2022: complications in ~20%, peritonitis 12%, "Renal dysfunction is common"; uterus "large, friable, and prone to rupture"; sepsis ~60%.

**C19. "Antibiotics alone cannot clear pus from a closed uterus"** → **VERIFIED, stated fairly**. Hagman 2022: "Antimicrobials alone for treatment of pyometra may reduce the disease and prevent its progression but does not result in uterine healing"; and "when … the cervix is closed, medical treatment is not recommended and surgery is the treatment of choice." Every published medical protocol pairs antimicrobials with a drug that removes progesterone action and empties the uterus (PGF2α/cloprostenol, aglepristone, cabergoline). The sentence is fair; it could be broadened: *"Antibiotics on their own never cure pyometra — they cannot empty a pus-filled uterus, and in a closed pyometra nothing can drain at all."*

**C20. "Medical treatment with hormones plus antibiotics exists only for selected cases — a young, otherwise healthy breeding bitch with an open pyometra and no signs of sepsis"** → **VERIFIED, one omission**. Hagman 2022: "Purely medical (pharmacologic) treatment can be an alternative in younger and otherwise healthy breeding animals with open cervix pyometra and without other uterine or ovarian pathologies"; not recommended with serious illness, peritonitis, organ dysfunction or closed cervix; also "or in a patient for whom anesthesia and surgery are hazardous" (omitted in the article — worth adding as it is a legitimate second indication). Merck: contraindicated if "systemically ill or endotoxemic".
Suggested addition: *"…or, occasionally, a dog for whom anaesthesia would be too dangerous"*.

**C21. "even then the disease recurs in a large proportion of dogs at later heats"** → **IMPRECISE**. Hagman 2022: aglepristone protocols "success rates of 46% to 100%, recurrence rates 0% to 48%"; Contri 2015 recurrence 17%; Fieni 2006 19%; England 2007 20%; Corrada 2006 21%; low-dose cloprostenol 85%; modified aglepristone protocol 0% at 2 y; mean long-term success ~86%. Merck: "prone to recur… most likely to recur during subsequent periods of diestrus". "Large proportion" overstates the typical 15–25 % (though up to ~50–85 % in some protocols). Hagman also stresses "Breeding on the subsequent estrus cycle is consistently recommended after medical treatment, to avoid recurrence."
Suggested: *"and even then pyometra comes back at a later heat in roughly one in five dogs (up to half in some studies), so the bitch should be bred at the very next heat and spayed once her breeding career is over"*.

**C22. "For all other patients surgery is the treatment of choice"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2018/2022.

## diagnosis.text

**C23. "a very high white blood cell count … [is] typical"** → **IMPRECISE (missing the important exception)**. Hagman 2022: "Leukocytosis, with neutrophilia and left shift, and monocytosis are characteristic findings". But Jitpean 2014: leukocytosis in only **54%** (121/223) of surgically treated bitches; and **leukopenia (3.6%) is the single most important danger sign** — 18-fold increased risk of peritonitis, 3.5-fold prolonged hospitalisation. Jitpean 2017: leukocytosis 62% open vs 89% closed. A normal or *low* WBC does not rule pyometra out and is worse news.
Suggested: *"a high white blood cell count is typical (and a low count is a warning sign of peritonitis)"*.

**C24. "raised kidney values are typical"** → **WRONG**. Jitpean 2014 (n = 356): increased creatinine in **4.8%** (11/228), increased BUN 6.5% (2/31). Jitpean 2017: creatinine and BUN "did not differ significantly between the two groups and most dogs were within the normal range." Merck lists high BUN/creatinine as *possible*. Hagman: "Renal dysfunction is common" — but that is proteinuria/concentrating defect, not raised creatinine. The commonest chemistry abnormality is raised ALP (37%), and anaemia (50%).
Suggested: *"a high white blood cell count and often mild anaemia are typical; we also check kidney and liver values, because the toxins can damage the kidneys and this affects anaesthesia and fluid therapy"*.

**C25. "confirm the diagnosis with an abdominal ultrasound, which shows the fluid-filled, enlarged uterus within minutes"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2022 (ultrasound detects intrauterine fluid even at normal uterine diameter); Merck ("thickened uterine wall, fluid-filled distended uterus"); Cornell.

**C26. "we scan any unspayed bitch who is unwell in the weeks after a heat, even without discharge"** → **VERIFIED** (good practice). Hagman 2022: "Pyometra should be a differential diagnosis in bitches and queens admitted with signs of illness after estrus, but the disease can occur at any time during the estrus cycle." Suggest "weeks or months after a heat" per C7.

## treatment.text

**C27. Ovariohysterectomy = same as spay but on a sick patient with fragile uterus** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2022: "The uterus may be large, friable, and prone to rupture, and it is important to handle the tissues carefully."

**C28. Stabilised with IV fluids and antibiotics before surgery; continuous monitoring** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2022 (supportive treatment, hospitalisation, "Intensive postoperative monitoring is essential"); Cornell.

**C29. "over 90 % of dogs recover"** → **VERIFIED (conservative)**. Hagman 2022: "the prognosis for survival is good and mortality rates relatively low, 3% to 20%"; Jitpean 2014 cites Hagman for "mortality is 3–4%" and found post-operative mortality **1%** (4/315); overall 10% only because 32 dogs were euthanised untreated; Jitpean 2017: 4.5% died; Hagman 2014 cats: dogs 3–4%. ">90 %" is safely true; "about 95 % or more" would also be defensible. Note mortality "can be considerably higher" with uterine rupture, peritonitis or septic shock (Hagman 2022) — worth keeping the "delaying surgery" sentence.

**C30. "once the uterus is gone the disease cannot return"** → **IMPRECISE**. Hagman 2022 lists "uterine stump pyometra" among complications and has a section: "A stump pyometra is when pyometra develops in residual uterine tissue in incompletely spayed bitches and queens, most often because of hormone-producing ovarian remnants."
Suggested: *"once the uterus and both ovaries are removed the disease cannot return (a rare 'stump pyometra' is only possible if ovarian tissue is left behind)"*.

**C31. "Delaying surgery is what turns pyometra from a routine emergency into a fight for life"** → **VERIFIED** as messaging (Hagman: early intervention increases survival; mortality much higher with rupture/peritonitis/septic shock).

## cost.text

**C32. Spay 483–753 € by weight; insurers LähiTapiola, Agria, Pohjola** → **UNVERIFIABLE / clinic's own data — treated as given** per brief. Internally consistent with the statement that pyometra costs more.

## recovery.text

**C33. "Most dogs are noticeably brighter within a day or two of surgery"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2022: "in uncomplicated cases 1 day to 2 days of postoperative hospitalization is usually sufficient"; "overall health status and most laboratory abnormalities improve rapidly after surgery and often normalize within 2 weeks." Jitpean 2017: 61% discharged within 1–2 days.

**C34. "go home with pain relief and usually a course of antibiotics"** → **IMPRECISE (stewardship)**. Hagman 2022: "The need for continued supportive care and antimicrobial therapy is evaluated several times daily on a case-by-case basis… Antimicrobial therapy is discontinued as soon as possible." Turin 2024: antimicrobials averaged 7 days, but the authors call for evaluating "outcomes without postoperative antibacterial treatment or with a shorter course". "Usually" pre-commits the clinic to a practice that current guidance discourages in uncomplicated cases.
Suggested: *"go home with pain relief and, if the vet judges it necessary (for example after peritonitis or sepsis), a short course of antibiotics"*.

**C35. "recovery suit or cone for 10–14 days… lead walks until sutures removed/dissolved at about 10 days"** → **UNVERIFIABLE (routine post-OHE care, no pyometra-specific source)**. Consistent with standard practice; acceptable as clinic instruction.

**C36. "repeat the blood test if kidney values were raised"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2022: "Persistent proteinuria and urinary protein-creatinine indicate renal disease that requires special attention"; concomitant cystitis/proteinuria usually resolve. Consider adding a urine check, since Hagman's marker is proteinuria rather than creatinine.

**C37. "Full recovery normally takes two weeks"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2022: laboratory abnormalities "often normalize within 2 weeks" (in uncomplicated cases).

## cat.text

**C38. "Yes, though less often than dogs"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2014 cats: 17 cats/10,000 cat-years (vs 199/10,000 dog-years, Jitpean 2012); 2.2% of queens by 13 years (Hagman 2022) vs ~20% of bitches by 10. Hagman 2022: less common "believed to depend on less progesterone dominance due to seasonality and induced ovulation".

**C39. "In cats the signs are the same — discharge, lethargy, poor appetite, increased drinking, a swollen belly"** → **IMPRECISE**. Hagman 2022 lists PD/PU, dehydration, tachycardia, abdominal pain among feline findings, so the list is not wrong, BUT: "Vaginal discharge may be absent or concealed by fastidious cleaning habits in up to 40% of affected queens", and 86% of queens have sepsis at presentation. Cats are also younger: median age 4 years (Hagman 2014), and breed effect strong (Sphynx highest; Siberian, Ocicat, Korat, Siamese, Ragdoll, Maine Coon, Bengal above 60/10,000).
Suggested: *"In cats the signs are similar — lethargy, poor appetite, sometimes discharge, increased drinking or a swollen belly — but up to 40 % of queens show no visible discharge because they clean themselves, so the disease is often advanced when noticed. Cats are typically younger (median 4 years) and some pedigree breeds (Sphynx, Siberian, Maine Coon, Ragdoll, Siamese, Bengal) are at higher risk."*

**C40. "cats hide illness well, so the disease is often further advanced when noticed"** → **VERIFIED in substance** (the 40 % concealed-discharge and 86 % sepsis figures above).

**C41. "prognosis with surgery is good"** → **VERIFIED**. Hagman 2014 cats: mean case fatality 5.7% ("slightly higher than … dogs of 3% to 4%"); Hagman 2022: 57% mortality only if uterine rupture. Optionally: "good, though slightly less so than in dogs".

**C42. "Spaying prevents it entirely"** → **IMPRECISE** — same stump-pyometra caveat as C30 (Hagman 2022 explicitly mentions stump pyometra in queens).

## prevention.text

**C43. "Spaying removes the risk completely, because the uterus is removed"** → **IMPRECISE**. Cornell: "spaying removes infection risk entirely" (lay wording). Hagman 2022: stump pyometra with ovarian remnant. Suggested: *"Spaying (removing uterus and ovaries) removes the risk almost completely — the only exception is the rare case where a piece of ovary is left behind"*.

**C44. "check her closely in the two months after every heat"** → **VERIFIED / slightly narrow** — see C7 (up to 4 months). Suggested: *"in the two to four months after every heat"*.

**C45. "avoid hormonal heat-suppression treatments, which increase the risk"** → **IMPRECISE** — see C15 (progestin effect not confirmed in the Finnish case-control; oestrogen mismating injections are the better-documented risk). Suggested: *"avoid hormone treatments to suppress heat or end an unwanted pregnancy, which are linked to a higher risk"*.
